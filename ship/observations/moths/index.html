Moths

Moths are my favorite thing to look for. They come in all shapes, sizes and colors, and there are far more species than butterflies. Some of them are active during the day, but most only come out at night. Every night brings different species. Over the years we have tried all kinds of ways to find and catalog them, and we have improved our methods over time. This page tells you all about those methods and some of the highlights of our moth-finding adventures.

It is important to note that we do not keep the moths we find. We photograph and identify them. Sometimes we catch them to examine them more closely, but we always release them back into the wild. We are simply very interested in finding and identifying as many species as possible.

01The white blanketBy night
Catch
Effort

A white sheet with a lamp in front of it. Preferably a bright and inefficient lamp, meaning that it should produce a lot of light in the UV spectrum. Moths fly to the light, land on the sheet and can be looked at, photographed and identified.

This is by far the most effective method, as it does not require a lot of specific materials. It already works well with a simple light. We use an old 160W mercury vapor lamp, which used to be the standard for street lighting. It is very bright, you should not look into it directly, or you will see spots for a while. UV lamps work well too, we tried this with some cheap lamps that are sold from overseas.

02The skinner moth trapBy night
Catch
Effort

One of the most beloved methods among moth enthusiasts. Simply put, it is a box with a ligh on top, and a funnel that leads the moths into the box. When flying towards the light, they fall into the funnel and cannot get out. Inside the box are egg cartons, which provide a place for the moths to calm down and rest. By morning, the moths are all asleep and can be photographed and identified. They are then released again, safely back into the wild and away from any predators.

On hot summer nights, the moths can be so numerous that they fill the box completely. We have had as many as over 80 different species in one night. The trap is very effective, but requires some time to set up and take down. On top of that, it is not very portable. You can buy these skinner traps online, but I built one myself for a fraction of the price. It is made from plywood, and the funnel is made from plexiglass.

03The LED bucketBy night
Catch
Effort

This is almost the same as the skinner trap, but more portable. The trap is made from a large bucket, with a UV LED strip on top. A funnel is placed into the lid and leads into the bucket, which is filled with egg cartons. Since the light is powered from a battery, it can be used anywhere.

The light is not as bright as other traps, but still works well. I made three of these traps, and we use them a lot because they are so easy to deploy. Because we can place them anywhere in the garden, we can attract moths that are very specific to certain plants or parts of the garden.

04SugaringBy night
Catch
Effort

This method consists of painting a sugary mixture on tree trunks or other surfaces. Moths are attracted to the sugar and will land on the surface to feed. Different mixtures exist, I have landed on a simple mixture of sugar and red wine, divided half and half. The sugar dissolves in the wine and can be painted on tree truns with a brush.

The most effective surfaces are rough tree trunks. The mixture seeps into the cracks and moths can land on it to feed. The method works well in summer, but also in winter. It almost exclusively attracts Noctuidae moths, which are more difficult to find with other methods.

05Butterfly netBy day
Catch
Effort

Walking around with a butterfly net allows for selectively catching moths you see flying around. This method is not super effective, but it is fun to do. It is also a good way to catch moths that are not as easily attracted to light, such as day-flying moths.

06Leaf mine searchBy day
Catch
Effort

There is a large group of moths that are so tiny that they are easily overlooked. They are also called micro-moths, and they are often very specific to certain plants. A large part of these micro-moths are leaf miners, meaning that their larvae live inbetween the layers of a leaf. They eat the leaf from the inside out, and leave a trail behind that is often very distinctive and specific in shape to the species. By looking for these trails, you can find the moths that made them. This is often more effective than trying to catch the adult moths, which are often very small and difficult to differentiate.

07PheromonesBy day
Catch
Effort

This method is very specific, and requires some research and intentional effort. Pheromones are chemicals that moths use to attract mates. By using a pheromone lure, you can attract the males of a specific species. This method is very effective, but should not be overused, as it can disrupt the natural mating process of the moths.

We acquired 8 pheromone lures for the Sesiidae family, which are specific to one or a few species each. This family of moths is very interesting. They mimic wasps and bees and are active during certain hours of the day. They are nearly impossible to find with other methods, but by using a pheromone lure, you can attract them from far away within minutes. They can sense the pheromone at very low concentrations.

08LuckBy day
Catch
Effort

Sometimes, you just get lucky. From experience, I can say that with some of the most interesting moths I have found, there was always some luck involved. Sometimes you just happen to be in the right place at the right time, and you see something interesting from the corner of your eye. Keeping your eyes open will result in more findings than you would expect.
